# What is it?
```
ɑː
```
The open back unrounded vowel (or **low back unrounded vowel**)

> **Key Distinction:** Length (ː) vs. Quality. Non-native speakers frequently mistake /ɑː/ as merely a lengthened version of /ʌ/ (as in _strut_) or /æ/ (as in _trap_). In reality, /ɑː/ requires a distinct pharyngeal retraction that completely alters its acoustic signature.

## Where is it pronounced?
* Its [place of articulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Place_of_articulation "Place of articulation") is [pharyngeal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharyngeal_consonant "Pharyngeal consonant"), which means it is articulated with the [tongue root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tongue#Structure "Tongue") against the back of the throat (the [pharynx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharynx "Pharynx")).
- It is [unrounded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roundedness "Roundedness"), which means that the lips are not rounded.

## Vocalic equivalent between ɑ: and ʕ̞
The open back unrounded vowel (represented as [ɑ], similar to the "a" sound in "father") is produced by lowering the tongue and pulling the tongue root back toward the throat (pharynx). If you narrow that pharyngeal space just a fraction more, the vowel transitions into a pharyngeal approximant [ʕ̞].
So, phonetically, [ʕ̞] has just enough narrowing in the pharynx to be classed as a consonant, whereas [ɑ] keeps the pharynx open enough to remain a pure vowel.
When linguists say that [ɑ] is the **vocalic equivalent** of [ʕ̞], they mean these two sounds share the exact same active articulators—namely, the tongue root backing toward the pharyngeal wall—just set at slightly different distances.

# How is it pronounced?
```horizontal

Phonetically, /ɑː/ represents a state of maximal acoustic resonance and minimal lingual obstruction in the oral cavity.
- **Tongue Position:** Pushed down and drawn back as far as possible into the pharynx without creating a pharyngeal fricative.
- **Lip Posture:** Fully neutral to slightly spread (unrounded).
```

# When?

## [[r-controlled vowels]] `word+r`
In late Middle English, pairs such as _cat_, _cart_, were pronounced [kat], [kart] respectively, distinguished only by the presence or absence of [r]. However, by the late 17th century they were also distinguished by the quality and length of the vowel.
 It has affected most varieties of contemporary English, which have distinct vowels in pairs such as _cat_, _cart_. In [non-rhotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-rhotic "Non-rhotic") accents, the /r/ of _cart_ has been lost; in Standard Southern British the word is pronounced /kɑːt/, distinguished from _cat_ only by the quality and length of the vowel.
When **a** is immediately followed by **r**, the **r** controls the pronunciation of the vowel. The usual short-a (/æ/) and silent-e (/eɪ/) patterns no longer apply. Instead, **ar** is pronounced as its own r-controlled vowel sound (/ɑː/ in BrE, /ɑr/ in AmE).

| Pattern | Example | IPA (BrE) | IPA (AmE) | What happened? |
|---------|---------|-----------|-----------|----------------|
| a | cat | /kæt/ | /kæt/ | Short a (/æ/) |
| a...e | cake | /keɪk/ | /keɪk/ | Long a (/eɪ/) |
| ar | car | /kɑː/ | /kɑr/ | R changes the vowel completely. |
| ar | park | /pɑːk/ | /pɑrk/ | Not /pæk/ or /peɪk/. |
| ar | star | /stɑː/ | /stɑr/ | R creates the "ar" sound. |
| ar | hard | /hɑːd/ | /hɑrd/ | R-controlled vowel. |
| ar | farm | /fɑːm/ | /fɑrm/ | R-controlled vowel. |

not **every** `a + r` sequence is pronounced /ɑː/. Common patterns like **are** (_care_), **air** (_fair_), and **ear** (_bear_) have different pronunciations (/eə/ in BrE, /er/ in AmE). Those are separate spelling patterns built on **ar**, but they don't follow the basic **ar → /ɑː/** pattern.

### Why does Carrot not have the long a sound?
This lengthening occurred when /a/ was followed by non-pre-vocalic /r/; it did not generally apply before intervocalic /r/ (when the /r/ was followed by another vowel). Hence the first vowel of _carrot_ and _marry_ has normally remained the same as that of _cat_. 

#### Then why do Barring and Starry have long a?
Inflected forms and derivatives of words ending in (historical) /r/ generally inherit the lengthened vowel, so words like _barring_ and _starry_ have /ɑː/ as do _bar_ and _star_.

## Broadening before Fricative `f`, `θ`, `s`
### Occurance rules
#### Rarity
- It is inconsistent and sporadic
	- Broadening affected Standard English extremely inconsistently. It seems to have been favored when /a/ was adjacent to labial consonants or /r/. 
	- It is apparent that it occurred most commonly in short words, especially monosyllables, that were common and well-established in English at the time broadening took place (c. 1500–1650).
	- Words of 3 or more syllables were hardly ever subject to broadening. Learned words, neologisms (such as _gas_, first found in the late 17th century), and Latinate or Greek borrowings were rarely broadened.

#### Sounds before which broadening occurs
- Broadening of a happens before preconsonantal or morpheme-final voiceless fricatives /f, θ, s/
	- The voiceless fricative /ʃ/ has never promoted broadening in Standard English in words like _ash_ and _crash_.
##### So why is Father broadened when it does not has any of those three sounds?
A particularly interesting case is that of the word _father_. In late Middle English this was generally pronounced [ˈfaðər], thus rhyming with _gather_ [ˈɡaðər]. Broadening of _father_ is notable both in two respects:
- its occurrence before an intervocalic voiced fricative [ð]
- its distribution in many accents that do not otherwise have broadening, such as those of North America.
The Oxford English Dictionary describes the broadening of _father_ as "anomalous" 
Dobson, however, sees broadening in _father_ as due to the influence of the adjacent /f/ and /r/ combined. _Rather_ and _lather_ appear to have been subject to broadening later, and in fewer varieties of English, by analogy with _father_.

#### Broadening spreading to its other forms
- Once broadening affected a particular word, it tended to spread by [analogy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analogical_change "Analogical change") to its inflectional derivatives. For example, from _pass_ ([paːs]) there was also _passing_ [ˈpaːsɪŋ].
	- In a phenomenon going back to Middle English, [f, θ] alternate with their voiced equivalents [v, ð]. For example, late Middle English _path_ [paθ] alternated with _paths_ [paðz]. When broadening applied to words such as _path_, it naturally extended to these derivatives: thus when [paθ] broadened to [paːθ], [paðz] also broadened to [paːðz]. This introduced broadening into the environment before a voiced fricative.
