[[Long Vowel]]


**Compensatory lengthening** in [phonology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phonology "Phonology") and [historical linguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_linguistics "Historical linguistics") is the lengthening of a [vowel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vowel "Vowel") sound that happens upon the loss of a following [consonant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consonant "Consonant"), usually in the [syllable coda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syllable_coda "Syllable coda"), or of a vowel in an adjacent [syllable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syllable "Syllable"). Lengthening triggered by consonant loss may be considered an extreme form of [fusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fusion_\(phonetics\) "Fusion (phonetics)") (Crowley 1997:46). Both types may arise from speakers' attempts to preserve a word's [[Moraic Weight]]

An example from the [history of English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_English "History of English") is the lengthening of vowels that happened when the [voiceless velar fricative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voiceless_velar_fricative "Voiceless velar fricative") /x/ and its [palatal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voiceless_palatal_fricative "Voiceless palatal fricative") [allophone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allophone "Allophone") [ç] were lost from the language. For example, in the [Middle English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_English "Middle English") of [Chaucer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoffrey_Chaucer "Geoffrey Chaucer")'s time the word _night_ was phonemically /nixt/; later the /x/ was lost, but the /i/ was lengthened to /iː/ to compensate, causing the word to be pronounced /niːt/. (Later the /iː/ became /aɪ/ by the [Great Vowel Shift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Vowel_Shift "Great Vowel Shift").)

### Explanation
# Loss of Consonants

## Definition

**Loss of consonants** is a historical sound change in which one or more consonants that were once pronounced gradually disappeared from speech. As these consonants were lost, the preceding vowel often became **longer**, and after the **Great Vowel Shift**, many of these long vowels changed into the pronunciations we hear today.

In other words:

```text
Consonant disappears
        ↓
Vowel lengthens
        ↓
Great Vowel Shift
        ↓
Modern English pronunciation
```

---

## Example 1: *night*

| Stage | Pronunciation |
|--------|---------------|
| Old English | /nixt/ |
| Consonant /x/ disappears | /niːt/ |
| Great Vowel Shift | /naɪt/ |
| Modern English | /naɪt/ |

The **gh** originally represented the velar fricative **/x/** (similar to the sound in German *Bach* or Scottish *loch*). When this consonant disappeared, the preceding vowel lengthened and later shifted from **/iː/** to **/aɪ/**.

---

## More examples

| Modern Word | Lost Consonant(s) | Modern Pronunciation |
|--------------|-------------------|----------------------|
| night | /x/ | /naɪt/ |
| light | /x/ | /laɪt/ |
| right | /x/ | /raɪt/ |
| might | /x/ | /maɪt/ |
| fight | /x/ | /faɪt/ |
| high | /x/ | /haɪ/ |

## Multiple consonants lost

Some words lost more than one consonant.

### knight

| Historical Form | Modern Form |
|-----------------|-------------|
| /knixt/ | /naɪt/ |

The following consonants disappeared:

- Initial **k**
- Medial **/x/** (written **gh**)

Only the spelling preserves their existence today.

---

### write

| Historical Form | Modern Form |
|-----------------|-------------|
| /wriːtə/ | /raɪt/ |

The initial **w** was once pronounced but later became silent.

---

### lamb

| Historical Form | Modern Form |
|-----------------|-------------|
| /lamb/ | /læm/ |

The final **b** was originally pronounced but later disappeared.

---

## Common silent letters caused by historical consonant loss

| Silent Letter | Examples |
|---------------|----------|
| gh | night, light, right, high |
| k | knife, knee, knight, know |
| w | write, wrist, wrong, wrap |
| b | lamb, comb, thumb, debt* |

> *Some silent letters (such as **b** in **debt**) were restored in spelling because of Latin influence rather than natural sound change.

---

## Why this matters

Many irregular English spellings make sense once you know that the spelling often preserves **older pronunciations**.

For example:

- **gh** in **night** is not random—it once represented a real consonant.
- **k** in **knife** was once pronounced.
- **w** in **write** was once pronounced.

Modern English spelling is therefore a record of the language's history rather than a perfect guide to its pronunciation.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Loss of consonants is a **historical sound change**, not a modern pronunciation rule.
- The disappearance of consonants often caused the preceding vowel to **lengthen**.
- Many of these long vowels later changed again during the **Great Vowel Shift**.
- Silent letters such as **gh**, **k**, **w**, and **b** are often remnants of these historical pronunciations.
