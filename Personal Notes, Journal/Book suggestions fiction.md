Yes, but with an important caveat.

Lionel Shriver is generally **not** a writer of romantic longing in the way Goethe is. She is much more interested in:

- obsession,
    
- identity,
    
- asymmetrical relationships,
    
- guilt,
    
- emotional dependence,
    
- moral ambiguity,
    
- and the stories people tell themselves about love.
    

So you won't find a modern _Werther_ from her. But you _will_ find books that explore adjacent psychological territory.

Here are the closest ones.

---

## 1. Big Brother ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (My top recommendation)

This is probably the closest to what you've been describing emotionally.

It's about:

- loving someone deeply,
    
- watching them become unreachable,
    
- trying desperately to save them,
    
- asking where love ends and self-destruction begins.
    

The relationship is between siblings, not lovers, but the emotional questions are remarkably similar:

> Can love become unhealthy?

> When do you let someone go?

> How much of another person's life can become your responsibility?

It is very compassionate by Shriver's standards.

---

## 2. The Post-Birthday World ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

This one is probably the closest if what fascinates you is:

> "What if life had taken another path?"

The novel literally splits into two alternate timelines.

One where the protagonist stays.

One where she leaves.

It explores:

- longing,
    
- imagined futures,
    
- emotional choices,
    
- roads not taken,
    
- the life that exists only in possibility.
    

Given everything you've shared about Nimmi, I think this book would resonate strongly.

---

## 3. So Much for That

Not romantic.

But one of the finest novels about:

- devotion,
    
- endurance,
    
- sacrifice,
    
- loving someone through suffering.
    

If what moved you in your own story was the quiet act of caring rather than romance itself, this is worth reading.

---

## 4. We Need to Talk About Kevin

You've mentioned this one before.

It's phenomenal, but not because it resembles your situation.

It's about:

- memory,
    
- guilt,
    
- retrospective narration,
    
- questioning one's own interpretation of the past.
    

Emotionally devastating, but in a completely different register.

---

## If I broaden beyond Lionel Shriver...

I actually think these match your recent conversations even more closely.

### Norwegian Wood

This is almost eerily aligned with:

- remembering someone years later,
    
- music becoming memory,
    
- quiet love,
    
- melancholy,
    
- accepting that some people permanently shape you.
    

---

### The End of the Affair

One of the greatest novels ever written about:

- obsessive love,
    
- memory,
    
- absence,
    
- trying to understand another person's interior life after they're gone.
    

---

### A Single Man

A very quiet novel about living after losing someone who fundamentally changed your life.

It isn't melodramatic.

It's contemplative.

---

### Stoner

Not a romance novel.

But perhaps one of the finest books ever written about loving deeply, living quietly, and carrying memories without theatrics.

---

## Based on everything we've discussed over the last few days...

Your reading taste seems to orbit a particular emotional constellation:

- _The Sorrows of Young Werther_ (idealized longing)
    
- _We Need to Talk About Kevin_ (psychological excavation)
    
- _Regretting Motherhood_ (difficult emotional truths)
    
- Dostoevsky (hyper-consciousness)
    
- and now this years-long reflection on Nimmi.
    

From Lionel Shriver specifically, I'd start with **The Post-Birthday World**. It asks "what if?" in a way that seems very compatible with how you naturally think.

If you're willing to step outside Shriver, however, **Norwegian Wood** is the novel I would put in your hands first. Not because your story is the same, but because Murakami has a remarkable ability to write about the way memory preserves people—not perfectly, but indelibly. That seems much closer to the emotional landscape you've been describing.
